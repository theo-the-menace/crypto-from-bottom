# Advanced — The philosophy of opening a trade

Opening a trade is not the same as pressing a button. It is a decision to exchange money for a specific risk. Before the order is sent, you should know what price relationship you are trying to use, what would prove the idea wrong, how much you can lose, and where you will take profit.

This article starts with the simplest trading idea: buy at a relatively low price and sell at a relatively high price. For a short position, the order is reversed: sell at a relatively high price and buy back at a lower price. The difficult part is that “low” and “high” are always relative to a timeframe and a risk plan. A price can be low compared with last week and still be high inside a larger downtrend.

## 1. The core is buying lower and selling higher

A trade needs a reason for the entry and a reason for the exit. A spot trader may buy BTC near a support area and sell near a planned resistance area. A trend trader may buy after a pullback in a rising market and sell when the structure fails. A range trader may buy near the lower boundary and reduce near the upper boundary.

These are different expressions of the same economic idea. The chart pattern, indicator, and order type are tools for choosing the relative price and controlling the risk. They are not the source of profit by themselves.

Do not confuse “buy lower” with “buy every falling coin.” A falling price may be cheap because the market has discovered a real problem, because the trend is still down, or because liquidity is disappearing. A reasonable entry needs context: a level, a structure, a condition, and an invalidation.

## 2. What counts as a trade signal?

A **trade signal** is a rule that changes your decision from “observe” to “consider an entry.” It should be an observable event, not a feeling.

A useful signal normally answers four questions:

- **Location:** Where is price? For example, near support, below resistance, or at a prior breakout level.
- **Context:** What is the higher-timeframe environment? Is BTC trending, ranging, recovering, or unclear?
- **Trigger:** What exactly must happen? For example, a 4-hour candle closes above resistance and the next pullback holds.
- **Invalidation:** What event proves the idea wrong? For example, a close back inside the range or a break below the retest low.

An indicator crossing is not automatically a complete signal. RSI recovering through 50, MACD turning upward, or an EMA crossover may support a hypothesis, but the signal still needs a location, a timeframe, and a risk definition. A signal that cannot be written in one sentence cannot be reviewed fairly.

Example:

> “If BTC is in a daily HH/HL structure, closes above the 70,000–70,500 USDT resistance zone, and a 4-hour pullback holds that zone while forming a higher low, I will consider a small spot position. A close below the retest low invalidates the idea.”

That sentence is a plan. “BTC looks strong” is an impression.

## 3. No stop, size, and target means no trade

Before opening a position, define three numbers or zones:

- **Stop / invalidation:** where the reason for the trade is no longer valid;
- **Size:** how much money is at risk if the stop is reached;
- **Target:** where you will reduce or close if the expected move occurs.

The stop should be connected to the idea. If you buy a support retest, the invalidation may be below the support zone or the higher low. If you buy a breakout, the invalidation may be a close back inside the old range. A stop placed only because it produces a convenient percentage is not a logical stop.

Size comes from the planned loss, not from confidence:

```text
position size = planned loss ÷ distance from entry to stop
```

Include fees, funding where relevant, and plausible slippage. A stop is an exit instruction, not a guarantee of the exact price during a fast move.

The target should be visible before entry. It may be the next resistance zone, a measured reward-to-risk level, or a rule for trailing the position. If the next resistance is too close to offer a reasonable reward for the planned loss, skip the setup. Missing a trade is cheaper than entering a trade whose numbers were unacceptable from the beginning.

## 4. Write the logic before sending the order

Before you click Buy or Sell, write a short record:

- What product am I trading: spot, margin, perpetual, or option?
- What timeframe defines the environment?
- What level or structure gives me the idea?
- What event triggers the entry?
- Where is the invalidation?
- How much can I lose in money, not only in percentage?
- Where will I take profit or reduce exposure?
- What news, liquidity, or execution condition could make the plan unreliable?

Then wait long enough to read the order ticket. Check the symbol, side, quantity, order type, limit price, stop trigger, leverage, margin mode, and estimated costs. A correct chart idea can still become a wrong trade because the wrong contract, side, or size was selected.

## 5. Record every opening and review it later

A trade journal is not a diary of emotions after the result. It is a record of what you knew before the result.

For each opening, save:

- a chart screenshot before entry;
- the product, timeframe, entry, stop, target, size, and order type;
- the exact trade hypothesis;
- the evidence that supported it;
- the condition that would invalidate it;
- the result after fees, slippage, and funding;
- whether you followed the rule.

Review trades in groups rather than judging one trade alone. Ask whether the signal worked in bullish, bearish, and range conditions; whether the stop was logical; whether the reward justified the risk; and whether execution costs changed the result. A winning trade can still break the rules. A losing trade can still be a good trade if the process was correct.

## 6. The decision to stay out is part of the philosophy

The market does not owe you an entry every day. Do not open a trade when the higher-timeframe context is unclear, the signal is late, the stop is too wide, the target is too close, the spread is abnormal, or you cannot explain the position in plain language.

The purpose of this philosophy is not to find a signal that never fails. It is to make every entry conditional, measurable, and survivable. The trade begins with a reason and ends when the reason is confirmed, invalidated, or no longer worth the risk.
